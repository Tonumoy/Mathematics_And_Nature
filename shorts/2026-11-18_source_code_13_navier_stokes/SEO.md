**Description:**
This swirl in your chai hides a million-dollar maths question. ☕🌀

Every drop of liquid and air follows one rule: the Navier-Stokes equations. They're Newton's F = ma, written for fluids: pressure pushes, stickiness (viscosity) slows, motion carries it along. Engineers already solve them on computers every day for planes, weather and blood flow.

The $1,000,000 Clay Millennium question is different: can a smooth 3D flow ever spin up to infinite speed in finite time ("blow up"), so the maths breaks? Stretch a swirl and it spins faster (spin × width² stays the same), like a skater pulling her arms in. Nobody had proved it either way since the prize was set in 2000.

On 8 Sep 2026, OpenAI said an internal AI system produced a proof that it can blow up when the fluid gets a smooth outside push (a forcing term, which the Clay rules allow). Mathematicians are still checking it, and the Clay Institute has not awarded the prize.

Everything you see is our own code: a ray-traced 3D cup, a real 2D Navier-Stokes solver (pseudo-spectral, 512²) that moves the milk; the force arrows on the drop are computed from that solve; the wing, storm and blood scenes use the same equations.
