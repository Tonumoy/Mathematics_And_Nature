Why Are Saturn's Rings So Flat? 🪐 Only 10 m Thick #shorts

## Description
Saturn's rings are wider than 20 Earths… and only about 10 metres thick. 🪐 Shrink them to a football field and they'd be thinner than a sheet of paper.
So what flattened them? Not gravity alone. The rings are billions of ice chunks, and every time two of them bump, they lose some up-and-down speed — but they keep their spin around Saturn. Bump by bump, a puffy cloud flattens into a razor-thin sheet.
In our simulation, 6,000 ice chunks orbit Saturn and collide: after each bump the up-down speed becomes v′ = −e·v (e = how bouncy a bump is). With e = 0.1 the cloud flattens; make the ice perfectly bouncy (e = 1) and it stays puffy.
Galaxies and young solar systems flatten the same way. Nature is running on code.

What you see: a ray-traced Saturn with the real ring radii (C ring, B ring, Cassini Division, A ring, Encke gap) and a real collisional N-body ring model, all drawn by our own Python code.
Sources: NASA Cassini — Saturn's rings (~10 m thick; ice from sand grains to mountain-size); JPL "Those Amazing Rings"; NASA Saturn fact sheet (ring radii); Brahic 1977 (A&A 54, 895); Goldreich & Tremaine 1978 (Icarus 34, 227); Bridges, Hatzes & Lin 1984 (Nature 309, 333). e = 0.1 is a model value.
