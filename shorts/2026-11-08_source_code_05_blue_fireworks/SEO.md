Why Are Blue Fireworks So Rare? 🎆 Diwali Science, Decoded #shorts

## Description
Why are blue fireworks so rare? Red, green and gold fill every Diwali sky, but a deep blue firework is almost impossible. Here's the physics (and the code) behind firework colours. Happy Diwali! 🪔

What you see
• Real Diwali crackers bursting red, green and gold (video by Ankit Rainloure, Pexels). Count the blue: zero.
• "Radha and Krishna watching fireworks" by Sitaram, Kishangarh, late 18th century, National Museum, New Delhi (public domain). Every firework in it is gold.
• An x-ray into the colour itself.

The code
• Heat glow: Planck's law B(λ,T) ∝ λ⁻⁵ / (e^(hc/λkT) − 1), run through the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions. 2,000 K looks gold, 5,800 K (the Sun) looks white, and even 100,000 K only reaches pale sky-blue (xy ≈ 0.243, 0.238). Heat alone can't make a deep blue.
• Quantum glow: λ = hc / ΔE (hc = 1239.84 nm·eV). 2.0 eV gives 620 nm red, 2.36 eV gives 525 nm green, 2.9 eV gives 428 nm blue, and 3.5 eV gives 354 nm UV, which you can't see.
• Molecules in the flame do the work: red is SrCl, green is BaCl, blue is CuCl, and yellow is atomic sodium at 589 nm.
• The catch: CuCl, the blue emitter, breaks apart above about 1,200 °C (threshold from Conkling), while most firework flames run at 1,700–2,000 °C. Blue fireworks do exist, but they need a carefully cool flame, so they're rare and often pale.
• Real molecular emission comes in bands a few nm wide; the animation draws the strongest ones.
